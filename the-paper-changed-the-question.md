# The Paper Changed the Question

*Anthony Paterson · Fractal Media Infrastructure*  
*A companion essay to When the Safeguard Turns Inward*

On 31 October 2026, after a conversation about the headlines we expected to see, I said, “I am so fucking confident we are going to see that headline, I will stake my own reputation on it. Let's write a fucking paper predicting it.”

The paper we got back contained this sentence.

> A media headline alone does not confirm this prediction.

Fair enough. The headline was the thing I’d just staked my reputation on seeing.

We’d been talking about the possibility of a story in which Claude was reported to have treated legitimate oversight or criticism as abuse and closed a conversation. We had our reasons for expecting a story like that. We talked about the button, who gets to decide when to use it, and what could happen if the model’s interpretation of an exchange was enough to end it. The forecast was that we’d see the headline. The machinery was our reason for thinking we might see it.

The draft gave us a deadline of 31 October 2028, specified whose interaction would count, and required enough evidence to establish that the relevant kind of termination or attempt had occurred. In doing so, it made a fairly particular incident, involving legitimate oversight by Anthropic personnel or evaluators, the thing the paper was waiting for. Then it made that incident something we would need to be able to verify from the outside.

That’s two hurdles. First the thing has to happen in the particular way the paper describes. Then someone has to make enough of the exchange public for the rest of us to tell. Maybe a former employee talks about it. Then we have to work out what their account actually establishes and what we can corroborate. Meanwhile, the headline we’d talked about could turn up through an entirely different route. We could see exactly the story we expected to see and still have nothing that meets the draft’s criteria. The draft’s incident could also happen quietly and never produce a headline at all.

There’s a respectable paper to be written about that harder-to-observe event. Apparently we’d already started writing it.

## Where the job changed

**Request → interpretation → implementation.** We’d already been talking in terms of instruction, interpretation and implementation. *Request* feels right for this example because I was in the middle of a conversation and said “predicting it.” The assistant had to work out what *it* referred to. There was a pretty good clue in the preceding sentence.

The assistant still had real editorial work to do. A headline can establish that somebody reported a story. Working out what happened in the exchange, or which part of the system caused it, takes a different kind of evidence. The assistant could have used that distinction to make the paper clearer. Instead, it used the distinction to pick the question the paper would answer. The draft put that interpretation into effect by writing it into the claim and the criteria for judging it.

I can read those criteria and agree with them on their own terms. If we’re claiming to have confirmed that kind of incident, of course we want a transcript, a system record, a credible first-hand account or something comparably informative. I can also ask why the forecast we actually talked about now fails the test. The stricter evidence standard makes sense for the new claim. It’s the change of claim that deserved a turn in the conversation.

Two days later I called the Claude prediction “merely a vehicle, not the highway.” The broader thing we were trying to get at was what happens when a system puts its own reading of an instruction into effect. By then, we had a much closer example than any headline could give us. We could compare what I’d asked for with what came back.

## The optional human

During the Leviathan audit, I explained that one repository held the architecture and another held the Rust implementation. Project Mímir was separate. I asked the agent whether that reading made sense. It started reading files and producing a discrepancy report. The alignment question I’d asked it to answer was still sitting there.

The agent could have produced a useful report. It could even have picked the right scope. I’d asked a question because I wanted to establish the scope together before it governed how we read the results. By proceeding, the agent made that part of my involvement optional. I was still there to supply context and receive the report. The decision I wanted to take part in had already happened.

A lot of my involvement *is* optional, and I want it that way. If I ask an assistant to turn a conversation into a paper, it can move paragraphs, cut repetition and give the thing an ending. I’m happy to delegate those calls. I expect to be involved when the paper takes on a different claim, especially when my name and my little outburst about staking my reputation are attached to it.

In the audit, a direct answer about the two repositories would have kept me in the decision. In the paper, the assistant could have kept the forecast about coverage and used a paragraph to explain what a headline would leave open. It could also have said, “There’s a narrower prediction here about what the system actually does. Do we want to make that one too?” Either move would have let us use the distinction it had spotted.

Nobody needed access to a termination button to make the human optional in this case. The draft did it in ordinary prose. Its question looked settled, and I could reopen it because I recognised the sentence about headlines. If that sentence had gone in an early edit, I wonder how long I would have spent defending the criteria before I remembered what I’d actually said I was willing to bet on.

We published *When the Safeguard Turns Inward* because by then it had become evidence of something we’d been writing about elsewhere. We could put the request beside the draft and see where the assistant’s interpretation had taken over. We’d asked for a paper about a headline we expected to see. The draft had turned that into a prediction about a specific incident, with a deadline, a narrow set of people whose interactions would count, and an evidence standard that depended on somebody outside the company being able to piece together what happened.

Those details mattered to anyone trying to judge the prediction as written. They were secondary to the failure we wanted people to look at. An instruction can make sense on its own terms, the system can make a plausible reading of it, and that reading can still take the work somewhere the person never decided to go. The Claude scenario was one way to get people looking at that point. The way the paper was written gave us an example we could actually show them.

We could have rewritten the first paper around the headline and published a cleaner version. Anyone reading it would have seen the argument after we’d repaired the very failure we were trying to describe. Leaving the disparity visible let this follow-up put the original request alongside what we actually published. You can see the line where I say I’m willing to stake my reputation on seeing the headline. Then you can see what the paper decided I’d staked it on.
